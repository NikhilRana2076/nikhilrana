How I build AI

5 principles
  1. 01

    Show the source

    Every answer should trace back to the evidence it came from. In my research, that meant the exact tagged fact in the filing.

  2. 02

    Let it say “I don’t know”

    An honest abstention beats a confident fiction. I measure both, and I never blend them into one number.

  3. 03

    Publish the uncomfortable numbers

    I report errors, abstentions, limitations and my own mid-study corrections alongside the headline result.

  4. 04

    Respect people’s data

    I build ethics in from day one, with a UK GDPR assessment and ethics approval before any analysis began.

  5. 05

    Make it reproducible

    I pin environments, fix seeds, log every model call and use spending guards, so anyone can rerun the work.

Detecting and Mitigating Hallucinations in Large Language Models for Financial Document Analysis: A Focus on UK iXBRL Reporting

Corpus: Tesco, J Sainsbury, AstraZeneca, Lloyds Banking Group, BAE Systems, Aviva, National Grid, The Sage Group, and four FRS 102 companies.

i.The question

Can an LLM be trusted with a company’s accounts?

In most documents, a hallucination is hard to pin down. In UK filings, it can be checked.

UK accounts are filed in iXBRL, where each number, such as revenue, profit or headcount, carries a machine-readable tag for its accounting concept, period, currency and dimension. When an AI invents a figure, it is making a verifiable false claim about a public record, not just an awkward sentence. That makes UK filings an ideal test bed for measuring hallucination precisely.

  1. RQ1

    How often, and how badly, do general-purpose LLMs fail on UK iXBRL filings without retrieval?

  2. RQ2

    Can SelfCheckGPT-style consistency checks, proven on free-text biographies, detect hallucinations in structured numbers?

  3. RQ3

    Does retrieval built on the filing’s own semantic tags beat both a naive baseline and detection alone?

ii.What I built

An end-to-end research pipeline

I built the whole pipeline myself. I chose a direct parser over the standard Arelle processor so runs would be reproducible offline, and I retrieved evidence by concept name rather than by answer ID, so the system had to find the answer instead of being handed it.

iii.Finding 1

The AI didn’t lie. It went quiet.

The biggest failure wasn’t fabrication. It was abstention.

Most research assumes hallucination means a fluent, confident wrong answer. So I classified every response three ways rather than two. Without retrieval, the models were rarely wrong, but they couldn’t find the answer more than half the time and said so. How long the filing was mattered more than how hard the question was.

52.0% vs 2.9%

Honest “not found” answers versus confident errors in the baseline. The baseline scored 29% on FTSE 350 filings and 86% on the much shorter FRS 102 filings.

iv.Finding 2

The hallucination detector was blind here

0 / 3

A SelfCheckGPT-style detector caught none of the baseline’s errors, on either model.

I extended it to compare numbers properly, so £1.2m, £1,200,000 and 1,200 (in £000s) all count as the same value. It still flagged nothing wrong. The only answers it marked “inconsistent” were ones that were actually correct. The pattern replicated exactly on GPT-5.6 Terra.

Reason 1 · Many true answers

“What was revenue?” can have more than one correct answer

Filings often tag several legitimate values for the same concept and period. When samples disagree, a consistency check can’t tell a valid alternative from an invented number.

ifrs-full:Revenue£73,712m ✓
tescoplc:RevenueFromSaleOfGoodsAndServices£72,886m ✓

Tesco, same period. Both are correct, and the £826m gap is insurance revenue.

Reason 2 · Systematic errors

The model was consistently wrong

Consistency sampling catches a model that is unsure and changes its guess each time. These errors came from misreading the same misleading evidence every time, so each sample repeated the same wrong answer and appeared to agree.

For example, one Lloyds question returned a net income figure when revenue was asked for.

vi.The honest takeaway

Accuracy can hide what matters

Retrieval mostly helped the model answer more. It barely changed how often it was wrong.

Coverage · abstention rate
52.0%→3.9%

Nearly every “not found” became an answer. This is where almost all of the gain came from.

Reliability · error rate among answers
6.1%→4.1%

This is a modest improvement, and at n = 49 and n = 98 I treat it as suggestive rather than proven.

For anyone deploying AI on financial documents, coverage and reliability need to be measured and reviewed separately. A single accuracy number blends them together.

Limitations I report openly

  • 12 filings and 102 questions, down from the originally planned 50 and ~500, with supervisor approval.
  • The baseline was cost-constrained to 8,000 tokens of context.
  • Reporting framework and company size are confounded in the corpus.
  • The planned independent dual review could not be completed because the external reviewer was unavailable.

Where I’d take it next

  • Scale the corpus towards 50 filings and 500 questions.
  • Build a detector that checks against all legitimate tagged values.
  • Test a finance-specialised model such as FinGPT or FinMA.
  • Improve narrative retrieval and dimensional disambiguation.
